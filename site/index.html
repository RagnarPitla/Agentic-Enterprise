Working paper

Why is there still no agentic enterprise? Yet.

Every large company now runs agents. None of them runs agentically. The gap is not model quality and not adoption. It is that an agent is scoped to a session, enterprise work is scoped to a commitment, and no system in the building stores a commitment as a durable object. The commitment cannot travel.

How this started

People assume anything this long came out of a machine. Here is how it actually went.

The thought came first, and it came from the work. I kept meeting companies that were deploying more agents and getting no more agentic, and I could not put it down.

Then I pointed five models at my own draft and told them to break it. They did. One found a number that cannot exist. One took my central claim apart using three citations I had to go and read before I would accept it. One found two hypotheses in the paper contradicting each other. Forty-one corrections are logged and two of them are to my own measurements.

What I did not do is ask a model what to think. The argument is mine, the mistakes were mine, and every decision about which objection to concede was mine. The corrections log is on the page so you can check that yourself rather than take my word for it.

The money was spent. The capability did not arrive.

Three answers get offered when someone asks why. Each one is testable, and each one fails.

The models are not good enough yet. This predicts that capability improvements convert into enterprise autonomy. Between 2023 and 2026 benchmark scores moved a great deal. The share of multi-week, multi-system enterprise processes running without a human in the loop did not move with them. A theory whose central variable improved by an order of magnitude while the outcome stayed flat has a problem.

Enterprises are slow. This predicts a lag, and lag predicts leaders. Cloud had leaders inside three years. Nobody can name the company that runs its order-to-cash agentically today. A diffusion curve with no leading edge is not a diffusion curve.

Governance is blocking it. This predicts that the constraint is permission. But the teams with the most permission, the AI platform groups running their own tenancy, have not produced it either. They hit the same wall the regulated teams hit, without the regulator.

All three answers share a shape: they say the thing is on its way and something is delaying it. The fourth answer is less comfortable and it is the one this paper argues. The missing piece is not a better agent. It is an object that does not exist.

An agent's memory ends when its session ends. Enterprise work does not end when a session ends. A purchase commitment made on a call in March is fulfilled by four systems over eleven weeks, and the only durable record of it is a sentence in someone's inbox. The agent was never the bottleneck. The commitment was never written down in a form any system could act on.

The argument

The paper is written as a disputation. A proposition seat states each claim at full strength; an objection seat attacks it at full strength. Where the objection wins, the claim is cut rather than softened. Three disagreements are left standing because neither seat could close them.

  1. Define agentic so it can be false

    Not "uses AI". A process is agentic at level N if it completes across N system boundaries without a human re-entering the loop to carry state between them. Five levels, measurable from logs, with the human role named at each rung. Most production deployments sit at level 1.

  2. State nine hypotheses with kill conditions

    Each hypothesis names the observation that would end it. H2 predicts a cliff at the first boundary crossing, not a slope. If the measured curve is smooth, the boundary claim is wrong and the paper says so.

  3. Measure what is actually measurable

    A census of 309 agent skills on one working machine. Load cost in real tokens, duplication, decay, dead links. The uncomfortable numbers are printed alongside the flattering ones.

  4. Run the objections at full strength

    Five frontier models were pointed at the draft with instructions to break it. Six claims died outright. Two of my own measurements turned out to be wrong, one of them arithmetically impossible, and both are now in the paper as evidence about the method.

  5. Name what would have to be built

    Six phases, each of which has to pay for itself before the next one is funded, because a migration that only pays off at the end never gets funded past phase two.

The strongest objection to the whole paper is still open, and it is stated as such: the economics may simply not justify any of this.

The three claims worth arguing with

If you disagree with the paper, these are the places to push.

  • Scope

    Every generation of this craft has been session-scoped

    Prompt engineering, harness engineering, loop engineering, context engineering. Four generations, one shared assumption: the unit of work fits in a session. Enterprise obligations run for weeks and outlive every session that touches them. The next unit is not a better session. It is a durable decision.

  • Sprawl

    There is no garbage collector, and there cannot be one

    The census found four distinct decay modes and no reclaim path for any of them. The library reached 309 entries because nothing is ever removed. You cannot collect a capability registry, because collection needs a reachability graph, and reachability needs durable objectives. Sprawl and non-agentic-ness are the same missing object showing two symptoms.

  • Concentration

    You did not choose hundreds of agents

    You chose hundreds of software vendors, and the agents arrived attached to them. Procurement is a real lever against twenty vendors and no lever at all against the tail of four hundred. This is the finding that broke the paper's original conclusion, and the revised one is weaker and more specific because of it.

Five levels, and where the deployments actually sit

The word "agentic" is doing too much work in every vendor deck, so the paper replaces it with a rung number that can be read off a log. The distinguishing question at each level is not how clever the model is. It is who carries state across the gap.

What one machine actually contained

Rather than cite a survey, I counted what was on the machine in front of me. One developer, two agent runtimes, a skill library that nobody planned to grow this large. Every number below comes from one script in the repository, and that script asserts six controls before it prints anything.

What holds up outside my own machine

A census of one laptop is evidence about skill libraries, not about enterprises. So the claims that matter most were checked against primary sources, and the ones that failed that check were dropped rather than softened.

An agent has already committed a company to a promise its systems did not hold

The paper's central mechanism is not hypothetical. It has been litigated. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, a chatbot told a passenger he could apply for a bereavement fare retroactively. The airline's own policy page said the opposite. The tribunal found for the passenger in negligent misrepresentation and awarded CAD 812.02.

Two paragraphs of that decision are load-bearing. At paragraph 27 the tribunal notes the airline's position amounts to treating the chatbot as a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions, and calls it a remarkable submission. At paragraph 28 it goes further, and this is the part that matters here: the airline could not explain why the page titled "Bereavement travel" was inherently more trustworthy than its chatbot, or why a customer should have to check one part of a website against another.

That is the grounding fix failing in court. Air Canada had the authoritative page, and had linked to it. The commitment was still formed at the conversational surface, and the law declined to privilege the system of record over it. Anyone whose plan is "ground the agent and add a disclaimer" should read paragraph 28 before relying on it.

Worth stating the limits: this is a small-claims tribunal, the sum is CAD 812.02, it is not binding precedent, and the tribunal never used the words authority, ownership or system of record. It is one documented instance, not a trend.

Six vendor catalogues, and the segment that is missing from all of them

The paper's diagnostic claim is that enterprise agents are named after systems of record rather than units of work. That was an observation about naming until it was checked against the vendors' own published catalogues.

The negative finding is the one that carries weight, because it is the one that could have come out the other way. Across all six catalogues, no segment is named quote-to-cash, order-to-delivery, or issue-to-resolution. Not one vendor organises its agents by a flow that crosses two systems. Workday goes furthest and indexes agents by which C-suite officer buys them, which is departmental segmentation written directly into the data model.

Oracle supplies the clearest instances: Payables Agent, Ledger Agent, Cash Processing Agent, Timecard Upload Assistant, Purchase Order Status Advisor. Each is named for the screen, table or module it operates on. ServiceNow's connector agents are the purest form: Kubernetes Pod Management AI Agent, Jenkins Build Management AI Agent, named after the integration rather than the work.

Honest limits: only the Oracle 207 and the Workday 21 are exact counts from enumerated tables. The rest are approximate, ServiceNow publishes no global list, and I did not personally recount all of them. The pattern does not depend on the precision of the totals, and the absent segment is a stronger signal than any of the counts.

The selection tax is measured, first-party, and larger than I assumed

My census could show that a capability index costs tokens. It could not show that the index degrades selection, because I had no accuracy data. Anthropic has published some, and it is an admission against interest.

The failure mode they name is the one my census found structurally: wrong tool selection, worst when names are similar. My library's worst pair scores an exact 1.00. So the degradation is not a theoretical consequence of having many options. It is a measured consequence of having many options described badly, which is a different problem with a different fix.

These are internal evaluations with no published methodology, sample size or confidence intervals. A 25-point gap is too large to be noise, but it is not a controlled result and should not be cited as one.

Somebody reached the authority argument before I did

Thoughtworks published an Agentic Scope of Authority Framework, framed around what an agent is legally authorised to commit the enterprise to, and reached for agency law to do it. That is the same problem this paper is about, arrived at by a different route and published first. The synthesis here is different, but the observation is not novel and it would be dishonest to present it as such.

Numbers I decided not to use

The figure circulating as "1.3 billion AI agents by 2028" is an IDC info snapshot sponsored by Microsoft, which its own footnote states. It is a vendor-commissioned forecast, so it is not independent analyst work and it is not a measurement of anything. I found no primary, methodologically-disclosed dataset quantifying enterprise agent duplication, abandonment or maintenance cost, which is why the argument here rests on a mechanism rather than a market size.

One number was too good to leave out, because it is self-refuting. Workday markets its Agent System of Record as a way for one team to govern thousands of AI agents. The published catalogue on the same page contains 21.

Four ways the library rots, and no reclaim path for any of them

This is the finding I did not expect to make. Every mature runtime that accumulates objects has something that removes them: reference counting, mark and sweep, a TTL, a retention policy. A capability registry has none, and the reason is structural rather than lazy.

There is a cheap test for this, and the paper recommends running it. Delete everything with zero invocations and zero inbound references for two consecutive quarters. If nobody in the organisation is willing to run that deletion, the unwillingness is itself the finding: it means nobody can say what any of it was for.

Commitments form in a narrow place and are fulfilled in a wide one

This began as an objection to my own thesis and ended up as the strongest argument for it. If an enterprise runs hundreds of software vendors, a proposal that depends on instrumenting all of them is dead on arrival. Procurement can lean on twenty vendors. It has no lever at all against the tail of four hundred.

Then the layers separate, and the picture changes.

That is the whole move. Instrumenting fulfilment is impossible; instrumenting formation is a conversation with two vendors. It is also the second independent argument arriving at the same design: the origin of a commitment is reachable and its destination is not.

The objection to this is conceded in the paper rather than answered. Putting capture in the communication layer hands the commitment graph to the communication vendor, which is a real concentration of power. So the ask has to be narrower than the capability: exportable, in a format the enterprise owns, or it is not worth having.

Enterprise systems write in ink. Commitments are made in pencil.

The objection seat scored a real hit here early on: commitment representation is not missing from the literature. ISO 15944-4 models it. Revenue recognition standards mandate performance obligations. So the claim "nobody has thought about this" is false and was cut.

What survives is narrower and harder to dismiss. Those models represent a commitment once it is confirmed. The interesting state is the one before that, and no system of record has a slot for it.

A provisional write is not a softer write. It is a write with an expiry and a named owner, which is a different object with different failure modes. The paper works through one of them honestly and cannot close it: a provisional write is a denial-of-service surface, because anything that can be created cheaply and must be reconciled by someone can be created in bulk.

Where this sits against the obvious alternatives

Compared with multi-agent orchestration

Orchestration frameworks genuinely solve routing, retries and tool selection inside a run. They are good at what they do. The boundary condition is the word "inside": the orchestrator's memory ends with the orchestration. An eleven-week obligation outlives every orchestrator that touches it, so the framework never gets to be the thing that remembers.

Compared with process mining and workflow

Workflow engines are the closest existing thing, and they already store long-lived state with owners and deadlines. The boundary condition is that they start from a modelled process. A commitment made in a sentence on a call has no process instance to attach to, which is exactly the case that leaks.

Compared with "just wait for better models"

This is the most likely objection to be right, and the paper cannot fully refute it. A sufficiently strong model with a long enough context could carry the state a commitment object would carry. What it could not do is make that state auditable, transferable between vendors, or survive the model being switched off.

Compared with one universal agent

The paper argues against its own title here. A single generalist that emulates every specialist is not obviously cheaper than the specialists, and the reviewers were right that capability parity is the wrong outcome variable. The claim that survives is about cognition and state, not about consolidating headcount into one process.

The positive claim, once: capture the commitment where it is made, give it an expiry and an owner, and let any agent read it. The agents you already own become useful the moment there is something durable for them to be about.

The experiment that would kill it

A thesis that cannot lose is not worth reading, so here is the shape of the result that ends this one. Take a real process and measure unassisted completion as a function of how many system boundaries it has to cross. The two hypotheses make visibly different predictions.

How to read it without taking my word for anything

The paper is opinionated and it is also a record of being wrong in public. Every claim carries one of five epistemic labels, so you can see at a glance whether you are reading a measurement, a citation, or an argument I am making up.

  • Every number traces to a script in the repository, and every script asserts controls before it prints
  • Forty-one corrections are logged with what was claimed, what was wrong, and who caught it
  • Six claims from the first draft were killed outright rather than softened, and the paper names them
  • Three disagreements between the two seats are left unresolved, because pretending to resolve them would be the lie
  • Where the reviewers overreached, the paper says so and defends the original position instead of conceding to be agreeable

Frequently asked questions

Is this an argument for one giant agent replacing all the others?

No, and the paper argues against that framing explicitly. Consolidating a fleet into one process is an infrastructure decision with its own costs, and a reviewer correctly pointed out that a single generalist emulating N specialists is not automatically cheaper than the specialists. What the paper claims is narrower: the reasoning about a piece of work should not fragment every time the work crosses a system boundary, and the state it depends on should outlive the session that produced it. Whether that runs in one process or forty is an implementation question.

Anthropic measured a multi-agent system beating a single agent by 90.2%. Does that not settle it?

It settles a different question. Read what the number is: a 90.2% relative improvement on an internal research eval, not a public benchmark, and the comparison is asymmetric, since the multi-agent side ran Opus 4 as lead with Sonnet 4 subagents against a lone Opus 4. More importantly, read the mechanism Anthropic gives for it. They attribute the win to token spend, not to expertise: token usage alone explains 80% of the performance variance, with tool calls and model choice accounting for most of the rest. The system is buying parallel context capacity. It is not demonstrating that specialists know things generalists do not.

Then look at what those subagents are. A lead agent decides at runtime how many to spawn, what each one is for, and what tools each one gets. They do not exist before the query and they do not survive it. That is one agent selecting skills and harnesses per task, which is the architecture this paper is arguing for, not the one it is arguing against. The target here is the static fleet: agents registered in advance, named after systems of record, each holding a standing grant, none of which any single query created or can retire.

Anthropic also documents the coordination costs honestly, and they are the sprawl failure modes in miniature: early versions spawned fifty subagents for simple queries, and three subagents duplicated each other's work on the same supply-chain question with no effective division of labour. Their own stated limit is worth quoting against any general claim, including mine: domains where all agents need the same context, or where dependencies run between agents, are not a good fit today.

The sharpest way to put it. That 90.2% was produced by a read-only system, whose subagents are spawned per query, hold no persistent identity or permissions, and cannot coordinate with one another. It is routinely cited in defence of agents that are build-time, department-named, separately governed and authorised to write. Neither Anthropic nor anyone else has offered evidence for that second configuration. The two things share a word and almost nothing else.

So the honest form of this paper's claim is not "one agent, never many." It is that parallelism across context windows is the only demonstrated reason to run a second agent. Specialisation on its own is not one, because specialisation is cheaper, more portable and more governable expressed as a skill selected at runtime than as a separately deployed and separately governed agent.

Multiple identities and multiple credentials mean multiple agents. Does that not sink the whole thing?

It sinks half of it, and the paper concedes that half. On the authority axis the objection is right: an entity that acts under many credentials is multi-agent in every sense a security review cares about, and the paper now separates the axes for exactly this reason. On the cognition axis it does not follow. A principal is not the same thing as an agent. One web server serves ten thousand users under ten thousand authorities without becoming ten thousand programs, and sudo -u does not fork a second mind. The authority argument constrains the design. It does not refute the claim about where reasoning fragments.

Is there an information-theoretic bound that says routing must degrade as the number of skills grows?

Fano's inequality is the usual candidate and it is worth being precise about what it says. Error probability rises with the number of options only if the evidence available to distinguish them stays bounded while the option count grows. That is a condition, not a law, and the interesting result is that the condition is currently satisfied: the census found hand-written prose descriptions, near-duplicate pairs scoring 1.00, and not a single typed precondition anywhere. So the bound supports the argument rather than refuting it, and it also names the escape route. Degradation here is contingent on the evidence being bad, and the evidence is bad because nobody ever had to make it good.

What is the strongest objection you could not answer?

That the economics do not justify it. The paper can show that commitments are not stored, that the storage gap explains the boundary failures, and that capture at formation is technically reachable. It cannot show that the value released exceeds the cost of building and maintaining the capture layer, the reconciliation process, and the human attention that provisional writes consume. Someone could accept every factual claim in the paper and still be right to do nothing. That objection is stated in the conclusion rather than buried, because it is the one that decides whether any of this gets built.

Why does the paper spend so long on its own mistakes?

Because the mistakes turned out to be evidence for the thesis. A parser bug silently truncated 187 descriptions while passing a non-emptiness check, which is the general lesson that a non-emptiness guard detects total blindness and never partial blindness. A headline number was arithmetically impossible and survived three separate frontier-model reviews because all three read the reasoning and none re-ran the count. A control assertion passed for the wrong reason. Each of those is a small instance of the same failure the paper describes at enterprise scale: a system that checks whether something is present, never whether it is right, because nothing durable records what it was supposed to be.

Where do people fit? Most of the discussion is about systems.

Two ways, and they are connected. First, seats are not capability. Bainbridge showed in 1983 that automating the routine parts of a job raises the skill floor for whatever exceptions remain, which is precisely the role the maturity ladder assigns to a human at level three. Buying licences does not create that skill and often quietly assumes it. Second, shadow AI is the same phenomenon as agent sprawl viewed from the other end. The organisation sees uncontrolled supply; the employee sees no usable supply and routes around it. The effect is that the rate at which commitments are formed goes up while the rate at which they are captured goes down, so the employee absorbs invisible risk and the organisation learns nothing.
